Benchless

You don't need a lab to publish your first paper.

I'm an undergraduate in Dhaka. My department has no fabrication facility and no research budget I could get near. I was one of three winners of the IEEE TCAD Hackathon 2026 anyway, on a device simulation I ran entirely on a laptop, and I'm a co-author on a paper accepted at IEEE EDTM 2026 where I did the machine-learning half.

Hozaifa · winner, IEEE TCAD Hackathon 2026 · co-author, IEEE EDTM 2026 · University of Dhaka, EEE

Most students in my situation get told to wait. Wait for a master's, wait for a funded group, wait until somebody hands you equipment. That advice costs people years, and in any field where simulation is already how the work gets done, it's just wrong.

The hard part was never the physics. It was not knowing what counts as a publishable question, how long a study should take, or which conferences are real. I got those wrong repeatedly before I got them right. Benchless is the part I wish someone had handed me.

What it does

  • You describe your field, your background, and what software you can actually get hold of. It comes back with simulation studies that are small enough to finish and still worth writing up.
  • It checks whether a conference or journal is indexed anywhere real before you spend a year aiming at it, and shows you the record it checked so you can verify it yourself.
  • It gives you a submission timeline with real dates on it, the sections reviewers in your subfield expect to see, and a LaTeX template that matches the venue.

It will not write your paper. There's an industry that sells authorship: you pay, somebody else writes it, and it lands in a journal that prints anything. People get caught. When they do, the damage lands on the student, never on the service. Benchless teaches you the method and checks your work against what the venue asks for. What you submit is your own.
